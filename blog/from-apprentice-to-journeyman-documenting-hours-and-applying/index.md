# From Apprentice to Journeyman: Documenting Your Hours and Applying to Test

What counts toward the experience requirement, how to prove it, what the application usually asks for, and how long the approval takes so you can study while the paperwork moves.
[licensing](https://passyourmasterelectricianexam.com/blog/tag/licensing/) · [journeyman](https://passyourmasterelectricianexam.com/blog/tag/journeyman/)

## Key takeaways

- Most states ask for about 8,000 hours of supervised electrical work, often with a classroom component, before you may sit the journeyman exam.
- Hours are proved with employer affidavits, apprenticeship completion records or pay records; start collecting them a year before you need them.
- Approval typically takes four to eight weeks, and your eligibility usually stays valid for about a year, so apply as soon as you qualify and study in the meantime.
- Buying study material does not start the application; the board does, and only the board can authorise a test date.

The exam gets the attention, but plenty of qualified electricians lose months on the step before it: proving they have the hours. Boards do not take your word for it, employers close or forget, and an application missing one signature goes to the back of the queue. Here is how the process usually runs and how to keep it moving.

## What the requirement looks like

Most states require around 8,000 hours of electrical work under the supervision of a licensed journeyman or master, which is four years of full-time work, before you can sit the journeyman exam. Many pair that with classroom hours, commonly 576 to 900 over the same period, which a registered apprenticeship provides. Some states accept a trade school or associate degree in place of part of the field hours, and a few have lower totals for a residential-only license. Your state page on this site lists the local numbers; the board's website has the official version.

## What counts

Hours generally count when they are spent on electrical installation, maintenance or repair under a licensed supervisor, on jobs that required a permit or inspection or were done by a licensed contractor. Hours as a helper on a non-electrical crew, low-voltage work in many states, and work done without supervision usually do not. Military electrical experience often counts in full or in part with a service record. Out-of-state hours count if they can be documented the same way.

## How to prove it

Boards accept some combination of:

- **Apprenticeship completion.** A certificate from a registered program, state or federal, is the cleanest proof and often satisfies both field and classroom requirements in one document.
- **Employer affidavits.** A signed statement from each licensed employer or supervisor giving dates, hours and the type of work, often on the board's own form and sometimes notarised. This is where delays happen: former employers move, close or do not reply.
- **Pay records.** W-2 forms or pay stubs showing employment with an electrical contractor, used to back up an affidavit or to replace one when the employer is gone.
- **Licence numbers.** The licence number of the supervising journeyman or master for each period, which the board checks against its own records.

Start collecting a year before you plan to apply. Ask each employer for the affidavit while you still work there, keep every W-2, and write down the licence number of whoever supervised you. If an employer has closed, the board will usually accept pay records plus a notarised statement from you.

## The application

Expect to provide: the experience documentation above; a government ID; the application fee, usually between 30 and 150 dollars; sometimes a photograph; and in a few states a criminal history disclosure or fingerprints. Some boards have moved entirely online; others still want paper and a wet signature. Read the checklist on the form twice and send everything in one package, because an incomplete application is returned rather than held.

## What happens next

The board reviews the application, which takes four to eight weeks in most states and longer at busy times of year. If something is missing you get a letter and the clock restarts. When you are approved you receive an eligibility letter, or the board notifies the testing vendor directly, and only then can you schedule a seat. The eligibility usually lasts about a year, which is time enough for a retake if you need one.

## Study while you wait

Apply the week you qualify, not the week you feel ready for the exam. The review period is study time: six to eight weeks is what most candidates need, and it fits inside the approval window. By the time the letter arrives you can book the earliest seat rather than the one two months out.

## After the pass

The vendor reports your result to the board. Some states issue the journeyman card automatically; others require a final licence application and fee, and a few require proof of insurance or a photograph. Two to six weeks is typical. You are a journeyman when the card or certificate number exists, not when the screen said pass.

## Keeping the hours going

Journeyman hours count toward the master exam in most states, usually one to four years' worth. Keep the same records from the first day: employer, dates, supervisor's licence number, type of work. The master application will ask for them, and the people who can sign for you now may be hard to find later.
