What to Expect on Electrician Exam Day, From Check-In to Your Score

A walk through the day itself: what to bring, what the check-in looks like, how the testing room works, how to pace the clock, and what happens to your result afterwards.

exam dayjourneymanmaster

Key takeaways

  • Arrive 30 minutes early with photo ID that matches your registration exactly, your eligibility letter, the code book edition your state names, and two approved calculators.
  • You will be photographed, patted down or scanned, and your code book will be inspected for loose notes before you sit.
  • Answer every question; there is no penalty for a wrong answer, and a flagged question you never return to is a guaranteed zero.
  • A preliminary pass or fail usually prints before you leave; the license itself comes from the board two to six weeks later.

Most candidates know the code better than they know the testing centre. The day has its own rules, and losing ten minutes to a surprise at the front desk is a bad way to start a timed exam. Here is how it runs at the computer-based centres that most states use, so nothing about the room is new to you.

The week before

Your confirmation email names the centre, the date, the arrival time and the references you may bring. Read it twice. If the address is unfamiliar, drive there once so you know the parking situation. Check that the name on your photo ID matches your registration character for character; a middle initial that appears on one and not the other is enough for some centres to turn you away.

Lay out the bag the night before: the ID, the eligibility or confirmation letter, the code book in the edition named by your state, any other reference the bulletin allows, two calculators that are non-programmable and silent, and nothing else. Phones, smart watches, notes, food and bags will be locked away or refused.

Check-in

Plan to be in the building 30 minutes before your appointment. Candidates who arrive after the start time are usually treated as no-shows, which means losing the fee and rescheduling. At the desk you will sign in, show ID, and have your photo taken. Many centres also take a palm scan or fingerprint that is checked each time you leave and re-enter the room.

Then the inspection. Staff will fan through your code book looking for loose paper, sticky notes and handwritten material beyond what your state allows. Permanent tabs and highlighting are fine almost everywhere; a page of formulas tucked inside the back cover is not, and some centres will confiscate the book rather than let you remove the page. Your calculators will be checked against the vendor's list. Pockets are emptied, and you may be asked to turn them out or pass a wand.

In the room

You will be walked to a workstation with a monitor, a mouse, a keyboard that is often disabled except for typing numbers, and a small writing surface. Scratch paper or a laminated board and marker is supplied; your own paper is not allowed. A proctor watches through glass or on camera.

The exam begins with a short tutorial on the interface. Take it even if you have used the software before, because the clock does not start until you finish it and it is the last calm minute you get. Learn where the flag button is, how to move between questions, and how the review screen at the end shows unanswered and flagged items.

Questions are four-option multiple choice. The clock sits in a corner of the screen. If your state scores the calculations portion separately, the software may lock you out of one section once you move to the next, so finish each part before you leave it.

Pacing

Divide the time by the number of questions before you walk in. Ninety questions in four hours is two minutes and forty seconds each, which is generous for recall questions and tight for lookups. Work in two passes. On the first pass answer everything you know without opening the book, flag anything that needs a lookup and move on. On the second pass work the flagged questions in order, giving each a fixed budget. A question that is taking more than three minutes is costing you two easier ones elsewhere; put your best guess in and flag it for a third pass if time allows.

Keep a checkpoint every 20 questions. If you are behind at the first checkpoint, speed up on the lookups rather than the recall questions, because lookups are where time disappears.

Breaks

Unscheduled breaks are usually allowed, but the clock keeps running, you cannot take anything out of or into the room, and you will go through the scan again on the way back. Use the restroom before you sit down and plan to stay seated.

The last ten minutes

With ten minutes left, stop looking things up. Open the review screen, make sure every question has an answer, and spend whatever remains on the questions you flagged as pure guesses. An unanswered question is worth nothing; a guess is worth a quarter.

After you submit

At most centres a preliminary result prints at the desk before you leave: pass or fail, and a breakdown by content area. That breakdown is the most useful piece of paper you will get if the result is a fail, because it tells you where to study next. The official result goes to your state board. The board issues the license, sometimes automatically and sometimes after a final application and fee, in two to six weeks. You are not licensed until that card or certificate number exists.

If something goes wrong

If the workstation freezes, raise your hand; the proctor can pause your clock while it is reset. If a question seems to have no correct answer, pick the best one and note the number on your scratch paper; vendors do accept comments after the exam. If you are sick, reschedule before the deadline in your confirmation rather than sitting the exam unwell. The fee for a late change is smaller than the fee for a retake.

The exam is a long day, but there is nothing in it that should surprise you now. Pack the bag, sleep, and go pass it.
