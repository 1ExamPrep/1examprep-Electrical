How to Study for the Journeyman Electrician Exam: A 6-Week Plan

Most people fail the journeyman exam on time, not knowledge. Here is a six-week plan that trains you to find answers in the code book fast, with a calculations block every week.

journeymanstudy tipsNEC

Most people who fail the journeyman exam do not fail because they don't know electrical work. They fail because the clock runs out while they are flipping through the code book. The exam is open book, and that is the trap: it tempts you to look everything up. The people who pass know roughly where the answer lives before they open the book.

This plan is built around that one skill. Six weeks, five or six days a week, about ninety minutes a day. If you have more time, stretch each phase; if you have less, keep the order and cut the practice volume, not the code drills.

Week 1: Learn the book, not the answers

Spend the first week getting physically fast with the National Electrical Code. That means:

  • Tab the book. Chapters 1 through 4 get the most traffic on the exam: definitions (Article 100), branch circuits (210), feeders (215), services (230), overcurrent protection (240), grounding (250), wiring methods (300 and the 3xx articles), conductors (310), boxes (314), and the big equipment articles in 400 (motors in 430, transformers in 450). Tabs are only worth it if you put them in yourself, because the act of placing them is the first pass through the layout.
  • Read the table of contents three times. Not the code, the contents. By the end of the week you should be able to say which chapter a question belongs to without looking.
  • Do the index drill. Pick 20 random terms a day (receptacle spacing, bonding jumper, ampacity correction, working space) and time how long it takes to land on the right section. Under 30 seconds is the goal.

Skip practice exams this week. They will only tell you what you already know: that you are slow.

Weeks 2 and 3: Chapters 1 through 3, one article at a time

Now work through the heavy articles in order, one or two a day. For each one:

  1. Read the article's scope and the first few sections so you know what it covers.
  2. Do a block of 25 to 40 practice questions that come only from that article.
  3. For every question you missed, write down the section number by hand. Not the answer, the section. You are building a map, not a list of trivia.

By the end of week 3 you should have covered 100, 110, 200, 210, 215, 220, 225, 230, 240, 250, 300, 310, 312, 314, and the common wiring-method articles (330, 334, 338, 342, 344, 348, 350, 352, 358).

Every week: the calculations block

Calculations are where the exam separates people, so they get their own standing appointment: two sessions a week, every week, from week 1 onward. Work the same families until they are automatic:

  • Conductor ampacity with temperature correction and adjustment for more than three current-carrying conductors (310.15 and its tables).
  • Voltage drop, single-phase and three-phase.
  • Box fill (314.16) and conduit fill (Chapter 9, Tables 1, 4 and 5).
  • Motor circuits: full-load current from the tables in 430, conductor sizing, overload and short-circuit protection.
  • Dwelling service and feeder loads (220), both the standard and optional methods.
  • Transformer primary and secondary current and protection (450).

Keep a one-page formula sheet that you rewrite from memory at the start of every session. You cannot bring it into the exam, but you can bring the version that is in your head.

Week 4: Chapter 4 and beyond

Articles 400 and up come less often but they are easy points when they do. Give a day each to flexible cords (400), switches and receptacles (404 and 406), appliances (422), motors (430), air conditioning (440), transformers (450), and a half day to the special-occupancy articles in Chapter 5 that your state tests on (hazardous locations in 500 through 516, health care in 517, swimming pools in 680). Chapter 6 and 7 get a skim, with attention to generators (445), emergency systems (700) and low-voltage circuits (725).

Week 5: Full-length, timed, no mercy

Now you take complete practice exams under exam conditions. Same number of questions as your real test, same time limit, phone in another room, the same code book you will bring on the day. Two full exams this week with a day of review after each one.

Review is where the score comes from. For every missed question, ask three things: did I not know where to look, did I look in the right place and misread it, or did I run out of time? Each of those has a different fix. The first one sends you back to the index drill, the second one is a reading-speed problem (slow down on the exception clauses), the third one means you are spending too long on the hard questions. Mark them, move on, come back.

Week 6: Taper

One more full exam early in the week, then stop taking tests. Spend the last three or four days on short sessions: the formula sheet, the index drill, and re-reading the sections your error log mentions most. Sleep. Eat a real breakfast on exam day. Bring two calculators and the exact edition of the code your state tests on.

The night before

Do not study. Pack the bag: ID, admission letter, code book with tabs, calculators, pencils, water. Read through your section map one time and put it away.

You already know this trade. The exam is a reading test about a book you have now read. Go pass it.
