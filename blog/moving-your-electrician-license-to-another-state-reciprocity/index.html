Moving Your Electrician License to Another State: How Reciprocity Works

What reciprocity actually means, the conditions nearly every agreement attaches, what happens when there is no agreement, and how to avoid the mistakes that turn a transfer into a full reapplication.

licensingjourneymanmaster

Key takeaways

  • Reciprocity is a board-to-board agreement to accept another state's license, usually for journeymen only and usually with conditions: a comparable exam, a license in good standing, and sometimes a minimum time held.
  • Where no agreement exists you apply as a new candidate, but documented experience almost always counts; you retest, you do not restart.
  • Master and contractor licenses rarely transfer because they carry business, insurance and state-law requirements.
  • City and county licenses in states without statewide licensing are a separate problem; a state license may not be enough there.

Electricians move for work more than most trades, and the license does not always come along. Reciprocity is the mechanism that lets it, when it exists. Here is what it is, what it is not, and how to handle the states where it does not apply.

What reciprocity means

A reciprocity agreement is a written arrangement between two state boards to recognise each other's licenses, so a journeyman licensed in one can get the equivalent license in the other without sitting the full exam again. Agreements are usually between neighbouring states or within a region, and they are specific: an agreement for journeymen does not cover masters, and an agreement between states A and B does not help you get into state C.

The conditions

Nearly every agreement attaches conditions, and the common ones are:

  • A comparable exam. You must have passed an exam the receiving state considers equivalent, which usually means a proctored, open-book NEC exam of similar length. States that grandfathered licenses without an exam are excluded.
  • Good standing. Your current license must be active, with no unresolved discipline.
  • Time held. Many agreements require that you held the license for at least a year, and some require that you obtained it by exam in the issuing state rather than by reciprocity from a third state.
  • Experience verification. Some boards ask for the same experience documentation as a new applicant, even under reciprocity.
  • A state-specific test. A few receiving states add a short exam on their own electrical law or amendments, even when they waive the code exam.
  • Fees and paperwork. An application, a verification letter from your current board, and the licence fee. The verification letter is the step people forget; order it early because some boards take weeks.

When there is no agreement

You apply to the new state as a new candidate. That sounds worse than it is: your experience almost always counts, because hours are hours, and the board needs only to verify them. You then sit the new state's exam. You are retesting, not restarting your career. Use the state page on this site to see what the new state's exam covers and which edition of the NEC it uses, because that is the thing most likely to differ from the exam you passed.

Master and contractor licenses

These transfer far less often. A master license in many states is tied to the ability to qualify a contracting business, and contractor licensing brings insurance, bonding, business registration and a business and law exam that is specific to the state. Even where a master reciprocity agreement exists, expect to take the business and law portion. If you plan to run a company in the new state, budget for the full contractor application regardless.

States without statewide licensing

A handful of states leave electrician licensing to cities and counties. A license from one city may not be recognised in the next, and a license from another state is not a city license. In those states you deal with the local building department, and the rules can differ ten miles apart. Find out which jurisdiction issues the license where you will actually work before you move.

The order of operations

  1. Confirm the agreement exists, from the receiving board's own website, not from a forum.
  2. Order a license verification from your current board.
  3. Gather your experience documentation anyway; some boards ask for it under reciprocity and all of them ask for it without.
  4. Apply to the receiving board with the fee.
  5. If a state-specific test is required, schedule it as soon as you are approved.
  6. Keep your original license active until the new one is in hand; letting it lapse can void the agreement.

Mistakes that turn a transfer into a reapplication

Letting the original license expire during the process. Assuming an agreement that existed five years ago still does, since boards add and drop them. Applying for a master license under a journeyman agreement. Moving first and applying second, so that the months of verification happen while you cannot legally work. Forgetting that the new state may be on a different NEC edition and that any state-specific test will be written to it.

If you have to retest

Treat it as a shorter version of the first time. You know the code; what you need is the new state's content outline, the right edition of the book, and three or four weeks of timed practice to bring the lookups back up to speed. The state study guide and online kit for the new state are built for exactly that gap.
