Pacing and Nerves: Managing the Clock on Exam Day

A time budget for a 100-question exam, the three-pass method, what to do when a question will not come, and the small physical habits that keep your head clear for four hours.

exam daystudy tips

Key takeaways

  • Work out your minutes per question before you walk in; for 100 questions in four hours it is 2 minutes 24 seconds, and the first pass should run well under that.
  • Three passes: recall first without the book, lookups second with a two-minute cap, calculations third.
  • A question that is taking more than three minutes gets a best guess and a flag; two easier questions are waiting.
  • Checkpoints every 20 questions tell you early whether to speed up, and the review screen in the last ten minutes catches blanks.

Updated October 22, 2026 · prices checked against the 1 Exam Prep store

Most candidates who fail know enough to pass. They lose to the clock, and the clock wins because nobody taught them to manage it. This post is the method: a budget, three passes, a rule for the question that will not come, and the habits that keep the budget intact when the nerves arrive.

The budget

Before exam day, divide the time by the number of questions. One hundred questions in four hours is 240 minutes, so 2 minutes 24 seconds each. Eighty questions in three hours is 2 minutes 15 seconds. Write the number down. Then set checkpoints: at question 20 you should have used no more than a fifth of the time, at question 40 no more than two fifths, and so on. Write those on the scratch paper the moment the clock starts.

The budget is an average. Recall questions should take 30 seconds; that is what buys the four minutes a load calculation needs.

Three passes

Pass one: recall. Go through every question in order. Answer anything you know without opening the book, and flag everything else. Do not look anything up on this pass, however tempting; the book is for pass two. A well-prepared candidate answers 40 to 50 percent of the exam on this pass in about a third of the time, which puts you ahead of the budget for the rest.

Pass two: lookups. Return to the flagged questions in order. Each one gets two minutes with the book. Go to the article from the table of contents, read the section to the end, answer, unflag. If two minutes pass without an answer, pick the most likely option, leave the flag, and move on.

Pass three: calculations and leftovers. Now the calculation questions and anything still flagged. Three minutes each. Write the formula before the numbers, use the table, round up. Same rule: over budget, best guess, move on.

The last ten minutes. Open the review screen. Every question must have an answer. Spend the remaining time on flagged questions in the order you think most likely to yield, and stop looking things up at two minutes to go.

The question that will not come

There is always one. You know you have seen the rule, you cannot find it, and five minutes vanish. The rule is simple and must be decided in advance: three minutes is the limit for any single question, then a best guess and a flag. Two easier questions are waiting, each worth exactly as much as this one. If you have time at the end, come back; usually you will see it in ten seconds with a fresh eye.

Nerves

The first ten questions feel worse than the rest. Expect that. Start with pass one precisely because it is the easy part; a run of recall questions answered quickly settles the hands. If the first question is a monster, flag it and go to the second. Nobody is scoring your order.

Breathing is not a cliché here. Four seconds in, four out, three times, between passes. It costs thirty seconds and buys back concentration that would otherwise leak for ten minutes.

The body

Four hours is a long sit. Eat a real breakfast with protein, not sugar, and nothing you have not eaten before. Water, but not so much that you lose ten minutes to the restroom and the re-entry scan. Use the restroom before you sit. If the chair is bad, adjust it before the tutorial ends, not during the exam. Dress in layers; testing rooms run cold.

Scratch paper

Use it for three things: the checkpoints, formulas written out before the numbers, and the list of flagged question numbers you most want to revisit. A clean sheet per calculation prevents the carry-over error where last question's current shows up in this question's answer.

Practise the pace

None of this works the first time you try it, which is why it has to be practised. Two full-length timed exams in the last three weeks, run with the three passes and the checkpoints, are enough for the rhythm to become familiar. On the day, the method runs itself and your attention goes where it belongs, on the questions.
