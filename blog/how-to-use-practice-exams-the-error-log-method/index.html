How to Use Practice Exams: The Error Log Method

Practice exams are not for finding out whether you would pass. They are for finding the code sections you keep missing. A method for taking them, logging misses by section, and turning the log into the last two weeks of study.

study tipsjourneymanmaster

Key takeaways

  • Take practice exams under real conditions: same question count, same time limit, same code book, phone in another room.
  • Log every miss by code section and by cause (did not know where to look, misread the section, ran out of time), not by topic.
  • Three causes need three different fixes: index drills, slower reading of exceptions, and a tighter time budget per question.
  • The last two weeks are spent on whatever the log names most often, not on another pass through the whole guide.

A practice exam tells you your score, and your score is the least useful thing on the page. What it can tell you, if you make it, is which sections of the code you keep getting wrong and why. That list is the study plan for your final two weeks. Here is how to build it.

Take the exam properly

A practice exam taken on the couch with the answer key nearby measures nothing. Set it up like the real thing: the same number of questions as your state's exam, the same time limit, the code book you will bring on the day with the tabs you will bring on the day, two calculators, scratch paper, and no phone in the room. Start a timer and do not stop it.

Work the way you plan to work on exam day: a first pass answering what you know, flags on what needs a lookup, a second pass for lookups, a third for calculations, and a final sweep to make sure nothing is blank.

Score it, then forget the score

Mark it against the key. Write the percentage at the top of the sheet and then stop looking at it, because it will not change what you do next. A 68 and a 78 need the same next step, which is the log, and a 90 on a practice exam has fooled plenty of candidates into easing off in the final week.

Build the log

For every question you missed, and every question you got right by guessing, write three things on a sheet you keep for the whole study period:

  1. The code section that answers the question. Not the topic. Not "grounding". The section: 250.66, or Table 250.66 Note 1, or 250.122(B).
  2. The cause. One of three:
  3. Location. You did not know where to look, or you looked in the wrong article and ran out of patience.
  4. Reading. You found the section and still got it wrong: you missed the exception, the condition, the unit, or you read the wrong row of the table.
  5. Time. You knew how to do it and did not get to it, or rushed it and made an arithmetic slip.
  6. One line on what the right answer was and why, in your own words.

After one exam the log has 20 to 30 lines. After three, it has 60 to 90 and a pattern.

Read the pattern

Sort the log by section. Three or four sections will appear again and again: 310.15 correction and adjustment, 220 demand factors, 430.52, 210.52 placement, box fill. Those are your final two weeks.

Now sort by cause.

Mostly location. Your problem is the book, not the code. Go back to the index drill: 20 terms a day with a timer until lookups average under 30 seconds. Re-tab the heavy articles if the tabs are not where your hand goes.

Mostly reading. You are answering from the rule and missing the exception, or picking the first row of a table that looks right. Slow down on the second pass. Read each section to the end before answering. Highlight exceptions in a second colour. On the exam, the ten seconds this costs saves the question.

Mostly time. You know the material and the clock beat you. Tighten the budget: two minutes a question on pass two, three minutes on calculations, and a hard rule that anything over budget gets a best guess and a flag. Practice a few sessions of thirty questions in forty-five minutes to make the pace feel normal.

The schedule

Six to eight weeks out: study by article, no full exams yet. Four weeks out: first full exam, then the log. Three weeks out: second exam, update the log, start the targeted work. Two weeks out: third exam. One week out: a fourth if the log is still growing, otherwise stop testing and spend the days on the sections at the top of the log and the formula sheet. Final two days: no new material.

Online kits versus paper exams

Paper exams in a study guide are good for the error log because you can write on them and keep them. Online kits add two things: randomised questions so you cannot learn the order, and an instant breakdown by topic so you see the weak areas without sorting by hand. If you have both, use paper for the first two exams and the online kit for timed drills in the last two weeks, when the score report by area is most useful.

What the log looks like at the end

By exam day the log should be a single page with a short list of sections and a note beside each one: "read the exception", "Table 430.250 not 430.248", "divide one-way length". Read it the night before, put it away, and walk in knowing exactly where your own traps are.
